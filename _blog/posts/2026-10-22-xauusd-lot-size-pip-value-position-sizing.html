Ask five traders "how much is a pip on gold?" and you may get three different answers. Confusion about XAUUSD contract sizes is one of the most common reasons beginners take positions that are far too big. This guide clears it up and gives you a simple formula to size every trade.

Pips and points: why everyone disagrees

Gold is usually quoted with two decimal places, for example 4,000.25. The smallest price step is $0.01, often called a point. The trouble is that different traders and brokers define a "pip" differently:

  • Many traders and signal providers call $0.10 one pip. With this definition, a move from 4,000.00 to 4,001.00 is 10 pips, and one pip is worth $10 per standard lot.
  • Some platforms treat $0.01 as a pip, which would make the same move 100 pips.
  • Others simply talk about a $1 move in price.

The safest approach is to think in dollars of price movement. Whatever anyone calls it, a $1.00 move on 1 standard lot is always $100 (on a 100-ounce contract). Convert everything to that and you can't get it wrong.

The position sizing formula

Professional traders don't choose a lot size first. They decide how much money they are willing to lose if the trade fails, then calculate the lot size that matches.

Lot size = Amount at risk ÷ (Stop-loss distance in $ × 100)

Where the stop-loss distance is the difference between your entry price and stop-loss price, in dollars of gold price.

Worked example

  • Account balance: $1,000
  • Risk per trade: 1% = $10
  • Entry: 4,000.00, stop-loss: 3,995.00, so the stop distance is $5.00

Lot size = 10 ÷ (5 × 100) = 0.02 lots.

If the stop is hit you lose about $10 (plus spread and any slippage), exactly the 1% you planned. If instead you had used a "standard" 0.10 lot without calculating, that same stop would cost $50, or 5% of your account on a single trade.

A wider stop means a smaller position

If your analysis needs a $10 stop on the same account, the formula gives 10 ÷ (10 × 100) = 0.01 lots. Your risk in money stays the same; only the position size changes. This is how you can respect gold's volatility without blowing up your account.

Margin and leverage

Margin is the deposit your broker holds while a trade is open. A simplified calculation:

Margin = Price × 100 × Lots ÷ Leverage

At a gold price of 4,000, a 0.02 lot position with 1:100 leverage needs about 4,000 × 100 × 0.02 ÷ 100 = $80 of margin.

Important: margin is not your risk. Your risk is set by your stop-loss and lot size. High leverage only makes it easier to open positions that are too large, which is why risk-based sizing is non-negotiable.

Quick checklist before every gold trade

  1. Decide your risk in money (for example, 0.5%–1% of your balance).
  2. Find a logical stop-loss level based on market structure, not a random number.
  3. Calculate the lot size with the formula above.
  4. Add a buffer for spread, especially near news or during quiet hours.

Frequently asked questions

How much is 1 pip on gold?

It depends on how a pip is defined. Many traders call $0.10 one pip, which is worth $10 per standard lot. The safest way is to think in dollars: a $1.00 move on 1 standard lot (100 ounces) is $100.

How do I calculate lot size for XAUUSD?

Divide the amount you are willing to risk by the stop-loss distance in dollars times 100. For example, risking $10 with a $5.00 stop gives 10 ÷ (5 × 100) = 0.02 lots.

Is margin the same as risk?

No. Margin is the deposit your broker holds while a trade is open. Your risk is set by your stop-loss distance and lot size.
